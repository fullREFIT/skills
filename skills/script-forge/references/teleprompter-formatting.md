# Teleprompter Formatting

Apply during Phase 3 (Produce) and the read-aloud quality check. This governs how the spoken lines are formatted so the script reads cleanly off a scrolling teleprompter. It layers ON TOP of the annotated script. The annotations (`[DELIVERY:]`, `[SCREEN SHARE]`, `[HUMOR:]`, section labels) stay. The spoken lines between them follow the rules below.

## Output format

- One idea per line. Break lines at natural pause points, never mid-thought.
- A line is what the speaker says before a natural breath.
- One blank line between each beat. A beat is one to four lines on a single idea.
- Plenty of white space. Short lines. Narrow column.
- Spell numbers as words. "Four hundred sixty-six," not "466."
- After any hard name or term, add a short pronunciation note in parentheses, once.

## Sentence rhythm (the rule most scripts get wrong)

Vary sentence length. Natural speech mixes short punches with longer flowing sentences. A script where every sentence is three to eight words reads jerky and robotic, the verbal equivalent of a machine gun, and it is the most common failure mode of "write short sentences" advice taken too far.

Cap sentences at about thirty words, but range freely below that. Put a two-word punch next to a twenty-five-word sentence that breathes. "One idea per line" is a formatting rule for the eye, not a license to make every sentence tiny. A long sentence still gets one idea per line, it just wraps across two or three lines at natural pause points.

Connect with tension, not sequence. Link ideas with but, so, yet, therefore, instead of a flat "and then." Kane Kallaway's retention checklist calls for a "but" or "therefore" roughly every minute of a long video. Tension between sentences is what keeps a viewer leaning in.

## Write for the ear

- Sentences vary in length, one idea each.
- Contractions. Active voice. Simple words.
- Talk to one person. "You," not "you all."
- Open on the problem or the promise, not a throat-clear.
- The CTA sounds spoken, not written.

## Cue density

Cues go on their own line, in brackets, and stay rare. Roughly one delivery cue every three to five beats. More for high-stakes pieces, fewer for casual ones. Compatible cues: `...` (short beat), `[pause]`, `[slower]`, `[faster]`, `[calm]`, `[matter-of-fact]`, `[serious]`. ALL CAPS on a single word means hit it harder, used rarely. A short sentence is its own emphasis.

Never use an em-dash, as a cue or anywhere else. This matches the voice rules.

## Do not overcorrect

Conversational is not sloppy. The script should be cleaner than raw speech, not messier. Cut rambling, repeated setup, filler, tangents, and half-finished thoughts.

## Read-aloud pass (run before delivering)

- Do sentence lengths vary, or is everything uniformly short? Uniform short is a fail.
- Are ideas linked with tension (but, so, therefore), not flat sequence?
- Could the speaker say each line on one comfortable breath?
- Does each line carry one idea?
- Is every written-sounding line gone?
- Are numbers spelled out and hard names noted?
- Are cues rare and only where they help?

If a line is hard to say, rewrite it.

## Prompter-ready version (on request)

When asked for "the prompter version," output a stripped file: spoken lines only, every annotation, label, cue block, and bracket removed, one idea per line, paste-ready into a teleprompter app. Keep the line breaks and white space. This is a separate clean copy, not a replacement for the annotated script.
